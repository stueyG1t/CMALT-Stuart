### 3a Description

#### Developing and maintaining my knowledge

My work with learning technology is informed by the UK General Data Protection Regulation and the Data Protection Act 2018 (UK General Data Protection Regulation, Article 5; Data Protection Act 2018). These affect how I select and configure systems, determine what personal information is necessary, explain its use to learners and consider how long it should be retained.

When I joined the University of Greenwich, I completed the mandatory Cylix _Data Protection Briefing_ course on 17 January 2024, achieving a score of 94 per cent ([Appendix L – Cylix Data Protection Briefing completion record](./assets/media/dpb-certificate.pdf)). This built on General Data Protection Regulation and data-protection training undertaken in previous employment, as well as my experience of commercial projects involving personal and potentially sensitive information.

Formal training provides an important foundation, but I have developed much of my practical understanding by applying data-protection principles during system design. When beginning a project involving personal information, one of my first actions is to examine the existing data flow: what is being collected, why it is required, where it is stored, who can access it and how long it is likely to remain available. This is particularly important when an existing process is being digitised or redesigned. Information may continue to be collected because it has historically formed part of a process, rather than because it remains necessary.

This approach reflects the principle of data minimisation, under which personal data should be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for its defined purpose (Information Commissioner’s Office, no date a). It requires me to identify the minimum information needed to fulfil a defined purpose, rather than collecting additional information because it might prove useful later.

#### Applying data minimisation to the Digital Backpack

I applied these principles when designing the Digital Backpack’s Digital Capabilities Assessment. The assessment operates as a Shareable Content Object Reference Model (SCORM) package within Moodle and produces a substantial amount of information about how students perceive their capabilities across different digital domains. When associated with an identifiable Moodle account, the detailed results could create a strong personal profile of the student’s confidence, perceived strengths and areas in which they feel less capable.

Students can be protective of information about their capabilities and confidence. The purpose of the assessment is therefore not to provide the University with a detailed means of monitoring individual capability. It is intended to help the student reflect on their current confidence and identify an appropriate route through the Digital Backpack.

I considered what information the University genuinely needed to fulfil this purpose. For the current version, the SCORM package passes only the student’s overall score to Moodle. It does not pass the individual question responses or detailed domain-level profile. The overall score provides the information needed to recommend one of three pathways: Explorer, Navigator or Pioneer. The detailed profile is presented to the student for their own reflection and is not retained for institutional monitoring or performance management.

This decision limits the analytics available to me. Retaining detailed domain results could support a more granular analysis of student needs, but the possibility that information might later prove useful is not, in itself, sufficient justification for keeping it. The decision has therefore affected both the technical design of the assessment and how I can evaluate it.

The assessment operates within Moodle, so associated enrolment, completion, overall outcome and activity information is covered by the University’s established information and records retention schedules (University of Greenwich, no date). Working within an institutionally managed platform provides an appropriate governance framework, but it does not remove the need to consider whether all Backpack-related information must be retained for the full period available.

I have identified this as an area for further development. Students may complete the Backpack or cease using it while their enrolment and associated activity records remain within Moodle. I am therefore seeking ways of further minimising the information retained and exploring whether Backpack records could be reviewed or removed earlier when they no longer serve a defined educational or administrative purpose. Any change would need to be developed with the colleagues responsible for Moodle and institutional records management.

This has reinforced my understanding that compliance with an institutional retention schedule and active data minimisation are related but distinct responsibilities. The schedule provides the authorised institutional framework. My responsibility as the designer of the learning experience is to continue questioning what information the Digital Backpack genuinely requires within that framework.

#### Implications for Version 2

Version 2 of the Digital Backpack may provide more individually tailored pathways. This could justify retaining additional assessment information because it would have a defined and immediate purpose in personalising the student’s learning experience. However, personalisation should not automatically be treated as sufficient justification for collecting everything technically available.

Before introducing this functionality, I will need to review what additional information is necessary, how its purpose will be explained to students, how long it should be retained and whether the proposed use requires further specialist data-protection review. This does not mean that additional information should never be collected. It means that each item should be connected to a defined purpose and reconsidered when that purpose has been fulfilled.

#### Interpreting data protection for apprentices

In August 2026, I created the University of Greenwich e-learning module _Practical Data Protection for Apprentices_ (Smith, 2026). I developed the module using guidance and training materials published by the Information Commissioner’s Office, including its resources on personal data, more sensitive information, data-protection principles and individual rights (Information Commissioner’s Office, no date b).

Rather than reproducing the Information Commissioner’s Office materials unchanged, I interpreted them for University of Greenwich apprentices. The module uses plain language, workplace examples and practical scenarios to help learners recognise personal information, check what they are permitted to do with it, protect it while it is being used and report concerns appropriately. The examples reflect the fact that apprentices may encounter personal information both during their studies and in different types of work placement.

Developing the module required me to move beyond recalling legislative principles and consider how those principles could be understood and applied by the target learners. Being entrusted to create this resource demonstrates that my engagement with data-protection legislation includes helping students understand their own responsibilities in professional practice. It also required me to return to current regulatory guidance and consider how changes in legislation and terminology should be reflected in the learning materials.

> **Evidence availability:** _Practical Data Protection for Apprentices_ is an internal University of Greenwich e-learning module and is not currently available publicly. Access to the module and its associated video materials can be arranged for CMALT assessors on request.

#### Clarifying informed choice in LinkedIn Learning

Data protection also affected my work supporting the institutional use of LinkedIn Learning. During its implementation, I identified ambiguity in University guidance about whether staff and students were required to connect a personal LinkedIn profile to their University-provided LinkedIn Learning account.

Although linking the accounts could provide additional functionality, it was optional. I was concerned that unclear wording might lead users to share information because they believed that doing so was required. I raised this issue and advocated for clearer guidance. I was subsequently informed through internal University correspondence that the clarification had been endorsed by the University’s Data Protection Officer. As a result, the guidance was amended to make clear that connecting a personal LinkedIn profile was optional.

This experience reinforced that data protection is not confined to technical security or contractual compliance. It also affects how choices are communicated. An option cannot be regarded as meaningfully voluntary if users do not understand that they can decline it. I now examine the language surrounding account creation and integration more critically, alongside the underlying technical flow of information.

### 3a Reflection

Earlier in my career, I sometimes encountered data protection principally as a compliance requirement to be checked during implementation. My experience of developing the Digital Backpack, supporting LinkedIn Learning and creating apprentice training has strengthened my view that it should influence a project from its earliest stages.

In practice, legislation affects what I ask when designing learning technology: what information is needed, why it is needed, whether an existing collection practice remains justified, how choices are explained and when specialist advice is necessary. It also requires me to acknowledge tensions. Collecting less information may restrict evaluation and personalisation, while retaining more information may increase risk and reduce learner trust.

I do not regard myself as a data-protection specialist. My responsibility is to maintain sufficient knowledge to recognise the implications for my work, apply established principles within my area of responsibility and involve the University’s data-protection, records-management or systems specialists when a decision falls beyond it. This has moved data protection from being a final compliance check to becoming part of how I define the purpose and boundaries of a learning technology project.

## 3b – Understanding and engaging with policies and standards

### 3b Description

Engaging with policy development has helped contextualise and temper my technologically focused work. I am unapologetically interested in digital technology and its transformative potential, which has led to opportunities to contribute to innovative approaches during early-stage technology adoption. However, I recognise that for technology to deliver long-term value in education, it must transition from experimentation to acceptance. Policy development provides the structure and accountability needed to support this transition.

Early in my work at Greenwich, I reviewed existing guidance on the use of Artificial Intelligence. I noticed that the policy was written too broadly and encompassed all aspects of AI, rather than focusing on generative AI. I researched a suitable justification for a rewrite and contributed to the development of an updated policy that better reflects current practice and ethical considerations.

I also actively contribute to the University of Greenwich’s AI Special Interest Group (AI-SIG), influencing internal policy on ethical AI use in teaching and learning. My work also aligns with national frameworks such as the Jisc Digital Capabilities Framework (Jisc, 2018) and the UK Government’s Essential Digital Skills Framework (Department for Education, 2019), both embedded into the design of the Digital Backpack.

I have advised on the adoption of Microsoft Copilot, flagging privacy, bias, and censorship issues, and contributed to draft guidance that frames AI as a support tool rather than a decision-maker.

### 3b Reflection

Engaging with policy at both institutional and sector levels has taught me the value of timing and framing — policy recommendations gain more traction when supported by concrete evidence and aligned with current strategic priorities. The challenge remains to bridge the gap between fast-moving technology developments and slower-moving policy cycles.

The innovator in me is often frustrated by bureaucratic delays, but I have come to value the role of policy in promoting ethical and sustainable practice. Policy development has helped me understand the importance of balancing innovation with responsibility, ensuring that new technologies are adopted in ways that are inclusive, transparent, and aligned with institutional values.
