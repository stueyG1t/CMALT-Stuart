## 2b – Understanding of your target learners

---

### 2b Description

The University of Greenwich has a diverse student population with significantly different levels of digital confidence. Understanding these differences is central to how I design digital skills support for learners.

This has shaped the development of the **Digital Backpack**, particularly its Digital Capabilities Assessment. I designed the assessment as a low-barrier starting point through which learners reflect on their confidence and are directed towards appropriate development opportunities.

My approach to accessibility combines functional testing, reference to standards and user testing where opportunities allow. I review the system against relevant **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2** criteria, but do not regard technical compliance alone as evidence that a learning technology works well for its intended users.

For the Digital Capabilities Assessment, I have personally tested keyboard navigation and tested with Cascading Style Sheets disabled to check that the content and structure remain understandable without the intended visual presentation. I have also used accessibility facilities available on the standard Windows 11 Intune-managed university laptop. I have not undertaken structured testing with experienced users of screen readers or other assistive technologies.

Accessibility also informs the language and structure of the assessment. Questions use everyday digital language and avoid unnecessary complexity or assumptions about previous experience. Where language has proved unclear through feedback, I have clarified it rather than simplified or over-explained it.

An earlier version placed greater emphasis on judging skills and competence. Feedback and reflection made me concerned about the experience this could create for learners who already believed that they were “bad with technology”. I therefore redesigned the assessment around confidence and reflection rather than correctness. The resulting **Explorer, Navigator and Pioneer** pathways are developmental routes rather than grades such as beginner, intermediate or advanced.

**I have also undertaken small-scale user testing. Two student** workers **each worked with two students for approximately 15 minutes, observing where learners hesitated, misunderstood terminology or encountered navigation problems. This approach is lightly informed by Suchman's (1987) concept of \*\***situated action\*\*, which encourages attention to what people actually do when interacting with technology in context.

The testing also challenged my own assumptions. Some student workers had lower confidence in English, so I simplified the testing instructions and focused on a small number of observable questions. This reinforced that inclusive design applies to evaluation processes as well as the technology itself.

The Digital Backpack is already in use while continuing to be evaluated and developed. Moodle and **Shareable Content Object Reference Model (SCORM)** packaging place practical constraints on what can readily be changed, so I make proportionate improvements to the current version while using more fundamental findings to inform Version 2. This requires me to distinguish between a system that technically works and one that learners can understand, navigate and use with confidence.

### 2b Reflection

Understanding my target learners has shifted my approach from designing for an assumed average user towards designing for differences in confidence, language, previous experience and ways of interacting with technology.

One workshop experience reinforced the emotional dimension of digital confidence. A student became tearful after I demonstrated how generative AI could help her ask technical questions and receive explanations at an appropriate level. She explained that she had previously been frightened to ask questions for fear of ridicule. This reinforced that digital exclusion is not simply about access or ability. Confidence and psychological safety can also determine whether a learner engages.

The move from judgement-based assessment towards confidence and reflection demonstrates how this understanding has changed my practice. My original question was largely, _how can I identify a learner's digital capability?_ I now also ask, _what experience does identifying that capability create for the learner?_

User observation has made me more cautious about professional assumptions. Terminology and behaviours that appear obvious to me may not be obvious to somebody encountering them for the first time. Although the testing was modest in scale, observing learners exposed issues that technical testing alone could not.

This process has reinforced my awareness of the limits of accessibility testing. Checking keyboard navigation, structure and WCAG criteria is valuable, but testing accessibility features myself is not equivalent to understanding the experience of someone who routinely uses assistive technology. Recognising what my evidence cannot demonstrate is as important as recording what has been tested.

I have also learnt to distinguish between identifying a problem and immediately resolving it. Moodle and SCORM provide practical institutional solutions but constrain the design. Learner-centred development therefore requires judgement about which improvements should be made now and which should inform the next iteration.

Moodle analytics have added another dimension to this evaluation. Completion of the Digital Capabilities Assessment has not consistently translated into continued engagement with the recommended Backpack activities. This shows that successful completion of an individual tool does not necessarily mean that the wider learning journey is working. Alongside user testing, this is informing my thinking about onboarding, progression and subsequent pathways.

I now see understanding target learners as an ongoing process rather than something established at the beginning of a project. Standards, testing and professional judgement all have value, but need to be combined, where possible, with observation of real users and an informed response to what that evidence reveals.
