### Description

My specialist area is the strategic development of confidence-based digital capability within higher education. This combines pedagogic design, technical development and institutional implementation to help learners identify and develop their digital capabilities without treating digital competence as a fixed measure or deficit.

The principal example of this work is the **Digital Backpack**, a Moodle-based initiative that I have led at the University of Greenwich. At its centre is a formative self-assessment that allows learners to reflect on their confidence across different areas of digital capability and then directs them towards appropriate development opportunities. The approach is aligned with the Jisc Digital Capabilities Framework (Jisc, 2018) and the UK Government's Essential Digital Skills Framework (Department for Education, 2019), but adapts these frameworks to the particular context of Greenwich learners.

The Digital Backpack developed from an earlier concept based more directly on measuring competence. Through consultation, prototyping and testing, I became increasingly concerned that this could reinforce the very inequalities the project was intended to address. A learner with limited experience of university digital systems could interpret a conventional skills test as confirmation that they were deficient rather than identifying opportunities for development. This led me towards a confidence-based, formative approach informed by concepts of self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997), in which the assessment acts as a prompt for reflection rather than a test with right and wrong outcomes.

This distinction has become central to my specialist practice. The detailed results are presented to the learner through a personalised radar profile rather than as a mark. The profile is intended to provide a starting point for reflection and development, with learners directed towards scaffolded opportunities within the Digital Backpack. These include institutional resources and external learning content, including LinkedIn Learning, which I have also worked to integrate more closely with Moodle.

My role has included developing the educational concept and initial prototype, establishing the confidence-based approach, mapping the assessment against digital capability frameworks, designing the learner journey and coordinating its institutional development and evaluation. I have worked closely with colleagues with complementary expertise. In particular, Jingyang Ai has contributed substantially to the technical development of the production system and its integration into Moodle. Our collaboration has allowed the pedagogic concept and technical implementation to develop together rather than treating them as separate stages of the project.

Accessibility and usability have also formed part of this development process. Rather than relying solely on automated compliance checking, I have tested the assessment through keyboard navigation, without style sheets, and using Windows 11 accessibility facilities, alongside iterative testing with colleagues and users. This has reinforced my view that accessibility needs to be considered through the experience of completing the activity, rather than simply as a technical requirement.

The work has also moved beyond its original institutional context through professional dissemination. Jingyang Ai and I presented **Designing for Reflection: Digital Capability Self-Assessment** at the Association for Learning Development in Higher Education (ALDinHE) Conference 2026 (Smith and Ai, 2026). This provided an opportunity to share the approach with a wider learning development community and to position the work as a contribution to discussion about reflective digital capability assessment rather than simply as a Greenwich technology project.

### Reflection

Developing the Digital Backpack has changed my understanding of digital capability assessment. I initially approached the problem primarily as one of identifying skills gaps and directing learners towards appropriate resources. I now see the assessment itself as part of the learning process. How a learner is asked to consider their capabilities can influence their confidence, willingness to continue and perception of whether digital development is intended for them.

This has required a balance between pedagogic ambition and operational reality. During development, for example, I explored more complex scoring and differentiation approaches. Although technically achievable, these created additional configuration and administrative requirements that would have made the system harder to maintain and scale. I therefore moved towards simpler completion and progression mechanisms. This reinforced an important principle in my practice: technical sophistication is not necessarily an improvement if it makes a learning system less transparent or sustainable.

Early implementation has also challenged one of my assumptions about the learner journey. Analysis of Moodle completion data in August 2026 showed that, of 305 users, 207 (67.9%) had completed the Digital Skills Check, which acts as the gateway to the wider Digital Backpack. However, only 48 of those users had subsequently completed at least one further tracked activity. This represents a continuation rate of 23.2%, leaving 159 gateway completers (76.8%) with no further recorded completion (University of Greenwich, 2026).

I regard this as an important finding rather than simply a disappointing outcome. It exposed a significant gap between completing the reflective assessment and completing subsequent learning activities. The data alone cannot explain why learners did not continue, and understanding this will require further evaluation with users. It has therefore changed the questions I am asking of the project. Rather than assuming that completing the assessment will lead naturally to further engagement, I now need to understand what happens at that transition and what learners need from the Backpack at that point. This is influencing the next stage of evaluation and development.

Testing has similarly reinforced the importance of observing how people actually interact with a system. Technical testing can demonstrate that something works, but it cannot fully predict whether learners understand what is expected of them or whether the experience encourages them to continue. I have therefore moved towards combining technical testing, learner feedback, observation and Moodle usage data when evaluating development. This gives me a more useful picture of accessibility and usability than compliance testing alone.

The project has also changed how I understand institutional development. I originally underestimated the time required to move from a viable prototype to something that could operate sustainably within university systems. Working with colleagues across different specialist areas has made the Backpack technically stronger, but has also required me to relinquish some control over how quickly development takes place. I have become more realistic about the value of institutional scrutiny, technical maintainability and shared ownership.

If I were beginning the project again, I would establish the evaluation strategy alongside the initial design rather than introducing it primarily as the system matured. In particular, I would define measures for the transition between completing the self-assessment and engaging with subsequent development opportunities. This would allow us to evaluate not simply whether the diagnostic works, but whether it changes learner behaviour and supports sustained digital development.

The specialist expertise I have developed therefore lies less in producing a particular diagnostic tool than in bringing together **reflective assessment, digital capability, learner confidence and institutional technology** as a coherent developmental process. The Digital Backpack remains an evolving implementation of that approach, and the evidence emerging from its use continues to challenge and refine my practice.
