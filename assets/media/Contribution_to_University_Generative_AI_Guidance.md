# Contribution to University Generative AI Guidance

## Purpose

This appendix provides a redacted and proportionate record of my contribution to the terminology used in the University of Greenwich's published Artificial Intelligence guidance. It records the policy issue I identified, the recommendation I made, the institutional response and the resulting published wording.

The complete email exchange has not been reproduced because it contains personal contact details, copied recipients, signatures, informal correspondence and a separate accessibility issue that is not required as evidence for Core Area 3b.

## Context

In September 2024, I reviewed the University's AI guidance and identified that its broad use of the term "artificial intelligence" could obscure its intended focus on generative AI. Artificial intelligence is an umbrella term that can include expert systems, search functionality and technologies embedded in library databases and other digital services.

Without a clearer definition, staff and students could interpret the guidance as applying to every technology containing an AI component. I considered this a potential source of confusion when explaining acceptable use, disclosure and academic integrity.

## Recommendation

On 26 September 2024, following a discussion about the guidance, I sent a written recommendation to the University's Head of Digital Engagement & Change.

The relevant part of my recommendation stated:

> My suggestion would be a simple statement early on recognising the wide variety of AI but this documentation is referring to Generative Artificial Intelligence (Gen AI).

I proposed the following definition:

> Generative AI, sometimes called gen AI, is artificial intelligence (AI) that can create original content—such as text, images, video, audio or software code—in response to a user’s prompt or request.

The definition was drawn from IBM's explanation of generative AI (Stryker and Scapicchio, 2024). I selected it because it was concise, suitable for a non-specialist audience and clearly distinguished generative AI through its capacity to create content in response to a prompt.

## Institutional response

On 27 September 2024, the Head of Digital Engagement & Change responded to confirm that the recommendation had been acted upon. The relevant part of the response stated:

> For point 2 I have added the text into all our “AI for…” pages so it’s clear that’s what we’re covering on those pages.

The response also acknowledged that further work was needed:

> I agree we need to clarify the terminology around this as well as our guidance.

This response provides evidence that the recommendation was received, considered and used to amend the published guidance.

## Published outcome

An adapted form of the proposed definition was subsequently published at the beginning of each of the University's four "AI for..." pages:

- [AI for students](https://www.gre.ac.uk/ai-guidance/students)
- [AI for teaching](https://www.gre.ac.uk/ai-guidance/teaching)
- [AI for research](https://www.gre.ac.uk/ai-guidance/research)
- [AI for administration](https://www.gre.ac.uk/ai-guidance/admin)

These pages were checked on 22 August 2026.

The published wording states:

> Generative AI, sometimes called gen AI, can create original content—such as text, images, video, audio or software code—in response to your prompt or request.

The change clarifies that these pages are concerned primarily with generative AI rather than every technology that could be described as artificial intelligence.

## Effect on my practice

I use the University's central [AI guidance](https://www.gre.ac.uk/ai-guidance) as the institutional reference point in workshops for staff and students. I now establish the distinction between artificial intelligence generally and generative AI at the beginning of these sessions, before discussing acceptable use, disclosure, academic integrity or particular tools.
